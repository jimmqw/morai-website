Best AI Tools for Teachers and Course Creators in 2026

Differentiation, feedback drafts and question banks are worth automating. Grading, diagnosis and your course's point of view are not.

May 21, 2026 | 11 min read | By Morai

Teachers and course creators have a strange relationship with AI: the tools are genuinely useful for the parts of the job nobody enjoys, and genuinely dangerous for the parts that matter. This guide separates the two.

It is written for people who teach — a classroom, a cohort, a paid course — and who have to defend what they produce.

The real bottleneck is not lesson content

Ask a teacher where the week goes and the answer is rarely "writing lessons". It is marking, differentiation, parent communication, and re-formatting the same material for three ability levels. Those are the jobs worth automating, and they are the jobs AI handles reasonably well.

What AI does well, and what it should not touch

1. Differentiation is the highest-value use

Take one worksheet and produce three versions: a supported version with sentence starters and a worked example, a standard version, and an extension version with a transfer task. This is a fifteen-minute job that used to take an hour, and it is easy to check because you can read all three against the original.

The rule that keeps it honest: the learning objective must be identical in all three versions. If the supported version asks a simpler question, you have created three different lessons, not one differentiated lesson.

2. Feedback: first pass only

Drafting comment banks works. Giving the model a rubric, a sample of the student's writing, and an instruction to comment on evidence and structure produces a usable first pass — but it will be generic unless you force specificity.

A prompt that behaves: "For each of the three paragraphs, quote one phrase and say whether it supports the thesis, then suggest one revision." Generic praise disappears the moment you require a quotation.

3. Quiz generation needs a verification pass

This is the most common failure. A model asked for ten multiple-choice questions will produce ten questions, and one or two will have a wrong key or two defensible answers. Always solve the quiz yourself before shipping it; if you cannot be bothered, do not use generated questions.

4. Course creators: where AI earns its money

  • Transcript to outline. Record yourself explaining the topic, transcribe, then ask for a module structure. Your own words, reorganised.
  • Exercise design. Give the model the concept and ask for five tasks of increasing difficulty, then keep the two you would actually set.
  • Localisation. Translating course materials is a large, repetitive, checkable job — a good fit, provided a human reads the result.

5. What to avoid

Do not let AI write your course's point of view. A course is an argument about what matters and in what order. Delegating that produces material that is correct, complete and forgettable.

Do not generate student-facing examples with numbers you have not checked. A wrong worked example teaches the wrong thing and is remembered longer than a correct one.

Do not paste student work into consumer tools. Names, IEPs and safeguarding notes are personal data. Use a tool with a data-processing agreement, or strip identifiers first.

A realistic weekly workflow

  1. Monday: turn this week's worksheet into three levels.
  2. Tuesday: run the rubric-based feedback draft on one class set.
  3. Wednesday: generate a question bank for the topic, then solve it yourself and discard the bad items.
  4. Thursday: transcribe one explanation and turn it into a revision handout.
  5. Friday: nothing. The point of the week is that Friday exists.

How a teacher actually starts, in one week

The failure pattern is buying a subscription and then not using it. The working pattern is picking one task, doing it badly once, and fixing the prompt until it is good enough to repeat.

Start with differentiation, because it is the largest time sink with the lowest risk. Take the worksheet you are teaching on Monday and ask for three versions with an identical objective. Read all three. You will immediately see the failure mode — usually the "supported" version lowering the cognitive demand instead of scaffolding it. Fix the instruction, not the output, and try again on the next worksheet.

By the third week the instruction is stable and the job takes four minutes instead of forty. That is the whole return on investment, and it does not depend on which model you use.

Prompt patterns that survive contact with a real classroom

Give the rubric, not the adjective. "Give helpful feedback" produces praise. "Quote one phrase per paragraph and say whether it supports the thesis, then suggest one revision" produces something a student can act on.

Constrain the reading level. Ask for a target reading age and give one sentence of the current text as a sample. Without a sample, models over-simplify into something that sounds like a textbook from 1975.

Demand the answer key separately. When generating questions, ask for the key in a separate block so you can solve the questions first and then compare. This one habit catches almost every wrong answer.

Course creators: the economics are different

A teacher is paid for contact time; a course creator is paid once per learner and then carries support costs. That changes which AI work is worth doing.

For a course creator the expensive parts are content production and support, not marking. The useful applications are transcript-to-outline, exercise generation, and a support knowledge base that answers the twenty questions every cohort asks.

The trap is production speed outrunning quality. A creator who ships four modules a week with generated filler ends up with a course that has no point of view, and no amount of marketing fixes that.

What the research does and does not support

Claims that AI tutoring doubles learning outcomes usually come from narrow studies with tightly scripted content. Claims that it harms learning usually come from studies where students used it to skip the work. Both are true in their own setting, and neither is a general law.

The safe reading: AI is good at supplying practice and feedback at volume, and bad at deciding what a particular learner needs next. Use it for the volume; keep the diagnosis.

Assessment design: the one place AI is genuinely transformative

Not grading — design. Writing a good multiple-choice item is slow, and the skill is rare. A model that produces twenty candidate items on a topic, given the exact learning objective and the common misconceptions, gives you a pool to edit rather than a blank page.

The useful instruction is to ask for items that target a named misconception, then ask for the misconception each distractor encodes. Distractors built from real misconceptions are what separate a diagnostic quiz from a guessing game, and that is a design decision a model can help with but cannot make for you.

For open-response questions the same principle applies at a different level: ask for the rubric first, then the question. A rubric written before the question tends to describe qualities worth assessing; a rubric written after tends to describe whatever the question happened to ask.

Parent and student communication

This is the quiet win. Explaining a policy three different ways for three different audiences — a student, a parent, a colleague — is real work and low risk. Draft it, read it, send it.

The caution is tone. Generated messages default to a formal register that reads as cold in a parent email. Ask for a specific register and a specific length, and keep one sentence of your own in every message.

The honest limitation

Nothing in this guide fixes the biggest problem in education technology, which is that tools are adopted at the level of the individual and measured at the level of the institution. A teacher who saves four hours a week gets no additional planning time; a school that buys a licence reports usage statistics rather than outcomes.

If you are the teacher, the return is yours to keep: use the saved time for the part of the job that only you can do. If you are the buyer, measure the thing that matters — marking turnaround, differentiation coverage — rather than logins.

FAQ

Will students be able to tell the material was AI-assisted?

They will notice generic material, which is a symptom of using it badly rather than of using it at all. Material built from your own transcript and edited by you reads like you.

Is it acceptable to use AI for marking?

For formative feedback, yes with review. For summative grades, no — the accountability chain requires a named human making the judgement.

What about academic integrity policies?

Most institutions now separate "AI-assisted preparation" from "AI-generated assessment". Check your policy for the specific wording; the safe default is to disclose tool use in your course materials.

Which tool should I start with?

The one already approved by your institution, if one exists. Otherwise start with a general chat model and a single task — differentiation — before expanding.
